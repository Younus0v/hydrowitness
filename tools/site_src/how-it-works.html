How HydroWitness works.

When SCADA can't be believed, a person needs two answers fast: how much time is left, and whether the screen can still be trusted. HydroWitness gives both, and it can only read, never write.

Try the real dashboardSee the technology

1. A clock that still runs when SCADA doesn't.

From the tank level and a physical model of the pipes, HydroWitness estimates how many minutes until pressure at the system's critical point (the farthest or highest customer) falls below 20 psi.

The worst case assumes peak demand and gives no credit for water coming in until a witness shows it moving. The dashboard states which demand it assumed, and asks for a tank reading to re-anchor the clock.

If real demand is far above the configured peak (a main break, fire flow), the clock can be late. That is why the screen says what it assumed.

2. Three witnesses, one claim.

SCADA makes a claim. HydroWitness asks three independent witnesses whether it holds up, and counts them by how much each deserves to be believed.

An operator

Someone reads the real gauge, the sight glass or the pump itself and types it in. This is the strongest witness, because a person is looking at the equipment.

Customers

Three or more different addresses reporting low pressure inside half an hour. They can raise amber, but never red on their own, so a flood of calls can't be used to fake an emergency.

The Witness Node

A small box on the pump motor that listens to its vibration and sound. It can say the pump is stopped while SCADA says it is running, and the screen can't forge that.

3. Green, amber, red, in plain words.

The color is never the only signal: each state has its own shape and its own sentence, and the reasons are listed underneath. Once it turns red it stays red until a person clears it.

Green: screen and witnesses agree

Nothing to do. The clock and the readings are consistent with each other.

Amber: check the screen against the pump

Something doesn't line up. Go and look at the equipment before you rely on the screen.

Red: do not trust the screen

The disagreement is serious or a person confirmed it. Read the gauge and the sight glass yourself, then open the Lockout Playbook.

4. A small box that listens to the pump.

The Witness Node clamps onto a pump motor. An accelerometer and a microphone tell it whether the pump is running. At install it learns that pump's own signature in a short calibration, with the pump off and then on.

It reports on or off to the server in a signed message that can't be forged or replayed. It has no pressure sensor and no connection to the plant.

It is built from off-the-shelf parts: an ESP32 board, an MPU6050 accelerometer, an INMP441 microphone, a button, three lights and a buzzer.

5. What happens next.

A warning that doesn't say what to do is just noise.

Lockout Playbook

A checklist in four phases (Confirm, Contain, Prepare, Recover). Each step has an owner and a time target against the countdown clock, and ticking it off is recorded. It is a template: your own emergency plan comes first.

Private alerts

Alerts go only to a private, token-protected channel on a server the utility controls. There is no public alert path by design.

Tamper-evident log

Every reading, decision and action goes into a log where each record carries the fingerprint of the one before it. Editing, removing or reordering a record breaks the chain.

Drill Mode: practise before it matters.

Six attack drills run against the real engines on a simulated plant, so a team can watch what each one looks like and rehearse what to do. You can run them in your browser.
