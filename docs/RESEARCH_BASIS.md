# Research basis: what we cite, what we checked, and what we do not claim

Every source below was opened and read by a team member's tools in November 2026, and the facts listed are the ones the page itself states. A source that could not be opened is listed at the bottom as **unchecked**, and nothing in our claims depends on it. Before quoting any line in a submission, open the link yourself once: a page can change.

## 1. The problem is real, and it is on small systems

| fact | source | what we use it for |
|---|---|---|
| EPA says "over 70% of the systems inspected by EPA since September 2023" were in violation of basic Safe Drinking Water Act Section 1433 requirements. Issued May 2024; EPA, CISA and the FBI recommend eight actions, among them reducing exposure to the public internet, changing default passwords, and exercising incident response plans. | EPA, "Enforcement Alert: Drinking Water Systems to Address Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities", https://www.epa.gov/node/289709 | Basic cyber hygiene is missing in many water systems. The authority said the attackers did not reach its treatment plant, the affected equipment was a pump that regulates pressure to elevated areas, and there was no known risk to drinking water. | **Two small private water utilities in Colorado, late August 2026.** A spokesperson for the governor's office said the attackers "changed equipment settings, disabled remote access and alarms, and altered pumping cycles." Each utility serves fewer than 200 people. The disruptions were "brief and did not affect water services or public safety." The governor's office said only "foreign actors" and did not confirm any link to the Iranian-backed campaign CISA has described; "few technical details are available." | SecurityWeek, 21 Sep 2026, "Colorado water utilities hit by cyberattacks targeting OT systems" (reporting a Denver Post story we could not open). It is a real, very small system, and the report is thin on detail, so the drill is our own reconstruction of the pattern, not a replay of what happened. |

What these cases share with our design, honestly stated: each had a control system that something other than the plant's normal staff could change, and in the cases where the source describes the response, people fell back to what they could see and do by hand. That is the situation the Time-to-Trouble clock and the Lockout Playbook are built for. None of these incidents is evidence that HydroWitness would have detected it. That is a claim about the future that we have only tested in simulation.

## 3. Method references we rely on and how we used them

* **EPANET hydraulics through WNTR.** Validation V1 compares the clock against EPANET (Hazen-Williams) on 310 randomized tank-and-pipe systems, using the WNTR Python package. The software and the numbers are in this repository (`hydrowitness/sim/wntr_validation.py`, `results/v1_wntr_validation.md`). We have not added formal citations for EPANET and WNTR here because we could not open their publication pages in this session. Add them from the packages' own documentation before submission (see "unchecked" below).
* **Exact confidence intervals.** Clopper-Pearson for proportions and exact Poisson upper bounds for rates (`hydrowitness/stats.py`, unit-tested against textbook values and for the 95% coverage guarantee). We use these because with small counts a bare "0 of 200" is not a rate.
* **Property-based testing.** The Hypothesis library searched for inputs that break stated rules (`tests/test_properties.py`). It found two real defects that ordinary tests had missed; both are fixed (see `UPGRADE_NOTES.md`).

## 4. Unchecked: do not quote until someone opens the source

* NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 (Guide to Operational Technology Security): we could not open it this session.
* The EPANET and WNTR publication references.
* How many US community water systems serve 3,300 people or fewer (EPA SDWIS or EPA fact sheets). Any figure on our website or in the brief must come from a page opened on the day it is written.
* Any statement about what a specific utility's SCADA product does. Our simulator is generic.

## 5. How the sources map to our claims

See `docs/VALIDATION_MATRIX.md`: each claim lists which of these sources, which simulation result, and which real-world evidence (if any) supports it. The rule is simple: a source can show that the problem exists. Only our own tests can show that our software behaves as stated, and only a real pump test can show the Witness Node works on real equipment.
